Four things the Linux window does differently

Every screen below is drawn in the desktop client’s own stylesheet — desktop/src/styles.css compiled by the Tailwind the app uses, over globals.css, with Archivo, Archivo Narrow and JetBrains Mono carried in the file. A colour, a row height or a radius here is the one the app would draw, so the GTK title bar in 01 is being judged against the real chrome rather than an impression of it. Where a photograph of the real Debian build says it better, the photograph is here and is labelled as one.

01

The resize affordance

decorations: false plus a title bar drawn in React means the window has no edge to grab. The window is resizable — the WM honours _NET_WM_ACTION_RESIZE and clamps at the configured 880×560 — so this is a missing affordance, not a missing capability. Both routes are drawn. Hover the edges and the corners of the first one: the cursors are live.

Route A — keep the chrome, draw the grips

Eight transparent strips mounted beside Titlebar.tsx, each calling startResizeDragging(direction) on mousedown. They are siblings of the title bar rather than children of it, which is what keeps drag.ts from starting a move at the same time — the bug that file already records, and the one way this gets built wrong.

Strips Window Hover an edge

Rebuilt, not photographed — the strips do not exist yet. 1040×640 logical pixels, scaled to fit. Maximised removes every strip: there is nothing to resize, and it gives the close button its corner back for the throw-to-the-corner gesture that only works when the window is maximised.

The one real cost, and it is measured. The sheet is flush to the window’s right edge and its scroller is .scroll-slim, 6px wide. A 6px right-hand grip is exactly the width of the scrollbar it sits on, so dragging the thumb becomes dragging the window. The wheel, the trackpad and the keyboard are untouched, and the thumb is transparent until hovered by design — but it is a loss, and the answer if anybody reports it is a 6px scrollbar gutter under [data-platform="linux"] in styles.css, not a thinner grip. FINDINGS proposes 4px edges and does not mention the scrollbar; 4px would leave 2px of thumb, which is worse than taking the whole thing honestly.

Route B — give Linux native decorations

Six lines of JSON, and everything the window manager does comes free and correct: edge resize, snap, Alt-drag, the right-click window menu, and the user’s own button layout. What arrives with it is a title bar we do not draw, cannot theme, and cannot predict — it is the window manager’s, so it is a different bar on every desktop. Those are the four below, at the real heights.

Desktop Our buttons

Rebuilt. The GTK bars are drawn to the metrics each desktop uses — Adwaita 37px with close only, Breeze 30px with three — and set in the system face on purpose, because that is the clash being judged. Today’s build draws a second row of window buttons underneath, because Titlebar.tsx keys them on !isMac && bridge.native; the fix is isWindows && bridge.native, which is one word.

Take route A: keep the custom chrome and draw the grips.

The title bar is not decoration here — it is where a client for several Firetowers says which one you are in, and STYLE.md spends a section on it. Route B trades that for a bar we cannot style, that is a different bar on every desktop, and that adds 37px of somebody else’s furniture above ours; route A gives Linux the same window Windows already has for about forty lines. The one thing route B has that route A does not is the right-click window menu, which no one discovers either.

Costs
One component beside Titlebar.tsx, ~40 lines plus a cursor rule per direction. One platform !== "macos" guard. One subscription to the maximised state, so the strips go away when there is nothing to resize.
Gives up
The scrollbar drag on the window’s right edge. The right-click window menu. The user’s own button layout — on Linux our three buttons are always right-aligned in Windows order.
Still true without it
Double-click maximises, and every WM keybinding (Alt+F8, modifier-drag) already works. Nobody is stuck at one size today; they are stuck at one shape.
Rejected
A visible bottom-right grip. It is a 1990s affordance, and it would sit on the one corner the sheet rounds.
02

The theme a fresh Linux install opens in

The theme chain works end to end on Linux — measured, same binary, GTK the only variable. What differs is the default for somebody who has chosen nothing: tauri.conf.json’s "theme": "Dark" forces the webview dark on macOS and Windows, and on Linux it is a no-op, because tao reads the XDG portal instead of preferred_theme. A tauri.linux.conf.json does not fix that; only window.set_theme() at runtime would. theme.ts needs no change either way.

The desktop is set to and Firetower

Rebuilt. Light here is globals.css’s own light branch plus the desk’s three overrides in styles.css — it is a designed theme, not a fallback. Press is forced dark with the desktop in light to see what the one-line set_theme(Dark) would ship: it is not ugly, it is just an app that has ignored you.

And it ties to decision 1. If Linux were forced dark and took native decorations, a user on a light desktop would get a light GTK bar sitting on a dark app — the one combination where the seam is unarguable. Route A plus following the desktop is the only pair with no seam in it at all.

Let Linux follow the desktop. Write it down; change nothing.

theme.ts already says the product’s intent is system; the platform that deviates is macOS and Windows, where a config key quietly overrides a preference the person expressed to their operating system. Honouring the desktop is also the stronger convention on this platform, and it is the one choice that costs nothing and surprises nobody — a reporter on a light desktop who posts a screenshot to the issue will be posting correct Firetower.

Costs
Nothing. No config, no runtime call, no change to theme.ts. A paragraph in STYLE.md or the Linux notes saying this is chosen rather than inherited from what tao happens to do.
Passed on
set_theme(Dark) in setup() under cfg(linux), to match the other two. One line, and defensible — but it buys consistency between platforms by spending consistency with the machine, and the inconsistency it fixes is invisible to any one person, who only ever has one of the three.
Follow-up, not this release
Whether "theme": "Dark" should stay on macOS and Windows at all. Dropping it would make the whole product follow the system, which is what theme.ts was built for — and it would put some Mac users in a light Firetower for the first time. That is a decision about the product, not about Linux, and it should not ride in on a Linux pull request.
Worth doing whenever this is touched
Watch the portal’s SettingChanged signal, which tao already forwards, so a desktop flipping to dark at dusk takes Firetower with it. The matchMedia listener in theme.ts should already carry this — unverified on WebKitGTK, and worth one line in the harness.
03

When there is nowhere to keep the sign-in

On a minimal desktop, a tiling WM or a box reached over SSH there is frequently no Secret Service at all. remember() swallows the rejection and write() strips the token out of localStorage because a keychain interface exists — so signing in looks perfect, and the next launch is the password field again with the server still listed and nothing said. This is not Linux-only. A locked or refused keychain does the same on macOS and Windows, where it is silent today; Linux is only where it happens most.

What it should do instead

Tell them at sign-in, at the moment the write fails. Not on next launch: by then we no longer know whether the keyring broke or the entry simply is not there, the person is not holding the password any more, and the harm has already happened. The card is Dialogs.tsx’s — 26rem, the same overlay, the same footer on a hairline — because this meets that file’s own test for a dialog: there is something to do behind it. Press Sign in.

Rebuilt. The dialog is live — both buttons work, Esc and the scrim dismiss it the way Card does. The sentence under the heading is the whole of the design: a system-level absence, named, with the one real remedy and no pretence that a button could carry it out.

The same state on three platforms

A table beside REFUSAL in shape and in voice, keyed on platform, and like the microphone’s Linux case none of the three gets a deep link — there is no one pane to send anybody to, and a button that opens the wrong one is worse than a sentence that admits it.

A keyring that is present but locked — a gnome-keyring whose login keyring never got unlocked, which is the common case over SSH — arrives as a different D-Bus error and takes the same card with the Linux line reading “Your keyring is locked, so Firetower could not put it there.” One more string, no new shape. Not worth a fourth drawing.

And then it has to stay said

Somebody who chose Use it for this session should not be surprised at the next launch either. Two quiet places carry it, and neither is a new mark on anything.

While you are signed in — one row in the profile menu, which is already where what is true of you and this window lives. No dot on the avatar: it carries ember-waiting and the update mark already, and a third meaning on one 10px disc is how the ember stops meaning one thing. The row opens the same card.

At the next launch — the door that already exists for a token that stopped working (App.tsx’s adrift branch), plus one sentence saying why. Today this screen is the photograph above: identical, and silent.

The position, on the part that is actually a question

  1. 1Sign in anyway, for this session. The person has already authenticated; refusing to proceed would make the app unusable on a legitimate machine for a reason that is not theirs. The dialog is what makes it a choice rather than a silence, and the remedy is reversible — start a keyring, sign in again, it sticks.
  2. 2Do not offer an unencrypted token on disk. Not as a checkbox, not behind a warning. A fallback that is chosen once and then invisible forever is exactly the property that makes it the wrong answer, and “less safe” is not a thing a person can weigh at the moment they are trying to sign in. FINDINGS lists two better fallbacks if one is ever wanted — the kernel keyring, which does not survive a reboot, and an encrypted file of our own, which is a threat-model decision rather than a patch. Both are engineering decisions for later. A clear sentence is the right answer for this release.
  3. 3Do not block the quit. A confirmation on close saying the session is about to be lost would be a second dialog for a thing already said twice, on the one action nobody wants interrupted.

Build the dialog, and stop discarding the error. This is the one that should not ship without a fix.

Everything else on this page is a window that looks wrong. This one loses a credential and says nothing, on every platform, and the Linux build is simply where it will be found. The interface above is the whole of the design; what it needs from engineering is for remember() to stop swallowing the rejection and for write() not to strip a token that was never stored.

New surfaces
One dialog in Dialogs.tsx’s shape — its own file beside it, since this is not voice. One row in ProfileMenu.tsx. One sentence on the Connect door.
Named as an implication, not designed
remember() and updateToken() must return or raise the keychain outcome rather than .catch(() => {}), and write() must keep the token out of localStorage only when it is actually in the keychain. That is the fix FINDINGS calls the cheapest honest one, and nothing above works without it.
And one decision for whoever builds it
The next-launch sentence needs to know why there is no token. Two ways: hydrate() can tell “the keychain refused” from “no entry” — they are already different errors, since secret_get errors on the bus rather than answering Ok(None) — or the registry entry carries a flag written at sign-in. Prefer detecting it at launch: it needs no new field, and it is self-correcting. Somebody who installs a keyring between launches gets the plain door rather than a stale sentence about a machine that has since been fixed.
Out of scope here
Any fallback store. The dialog is the deliverable.
04

What degrades, and whether it looks broken

Three things do not exist on Linux and none of them is being built this release. The job here is to say what their absence looks like, and to design a substitute only where the absence reads as a fault.

Artifact posters — this one reads as broken

capture.rs compiles to SUPPORTED = false, so hasPoster never becomes true and every tile draws ArtifactShot’s plate: bg-line with a Shapes glyph. One of those in a transcript is fine. Here is a library of them.

Rebuilt, on the real .gal / .art / .ar rules and the real plate. The honest judgement: it reads as loading. Nine identical grey plates with a faint glyph is precisely what this screen looks like on macOS for the two seconds before the pictures arrive — so a Linux user waits for something that is never coming, and the second read, once they realise it is not moving, is “broken” rather than “not available here”. It is the only one of the three absences that reads as a fault.

The substitute is a control that already exists. The library has a grid and a list, the choice is already remembered in firetower.artifacts.look, and the list is the view that does not need a picture — an 84px plate beside a name, with no picture yet already written on the row. So: where the machine cannot photograph and the person has never chosen a view, the library opens as the list. The person’s own choice always wins, and a view already on screen never moves under them — the default is read once, when the key is absent. One sentence beside the filters says why, and that is the only new copy on the screen: “This machine can’t photograph a page, so these have no pictures.”

useExpectPoster() already exists to answer can this machine photograph, and already goes broken after two failures — on Linux, within a few seconds of launch, because the stub errors immediately. It has had no reader since the live-frame fallback was removed for the security reason ArtifactShot explains. This gives it one.

The tray, and the badge

What is missingVerdictWhat the absence looks like
The tray Invisible tray.rs is #![cfg(windows)], so nothing is compiled and nothing behaves differently: close still closes, minimise still minimises, and no screen in the app points at a tray. A Linux user is not missing a thing they can see the hole of — they have the window the Mac has, which also has no tray and says why. Nothing to design.
The dock badge Noticeable, and not alone set_badge_count needs libunity and a running Unity session, so it returns Ok and does nothing. Inside the window everything still works — the ember dot on the rail avatar, the panel counts. What is gone is the part that reaches you when you are not looking at Firetower.

The finding under those two, which is bigger than either. Ember answers one question — is something waiting on you — and the product answers it off-window three ways. macOS has the dock badge and the menu-bar island; Windows has the taskbar overlay icon and the tray. Linux has none of the three, and FINDINGS adds a fourth silence: a desktop notification goes nowhere without a notification daemon, and main.rs discards that error the same way the keychain’s is discarded.

Drawn from main.rs, tray.rs and the FINDINGS measurements; no number here is invented.

Design one substitute — the library’s list default. Defer the other two, and say so in the issue.

The posters are the only absence that reads as a fault, and the fix is a default and a sentence rather than a feature. The tray is invisible and needs nothing. The badge is noticeable, but the honest answer is not a substitute inside the window — the window is the one place the information already is. What it needs is for the issue to say plainly that on Linux today Firetower can only tell you something is waiting while you are looking at it, so the reporter tries it knowing that rather than finding it.

Costs
The library default: one useExpectPoster() read in the existing useState initialiser, guarded on the absence of the stored key, plus one sentence in the filter row. Call it six lines and one string.
Deferred past this release
A Linux tray (AppIndicator gives a menu, never a click — raise() would have to be rebuilt as a menu item, and GNOME ships no host for it by default). The com.canonical.Unity.LauncherEntry D-Bus signal for the badge, which no machine here could receive. Linux artifact capture via webkit_web_view_get_snapshot(), which FINDINGS makes the cheapest of the three implementations — it is the thing to build first when Linux stops being experimental, and it retires this whole decision.
Not a design problem, but it belongs in the same note
The Linux-only warning: constant POSTER is never used would fail a -D warnings job, and the .deb ships Categories= empty, which files Firetower under Other in menus that group. One line of JSON each.

Where FINDINGS and I disagree, and what I could not check

  1. 1The 4px strip width. FINDINGS proposes four edges at ~4px and says the strips “will not” eat clicks because they are outside the layout. Outside the layout is not outside the window: the sheet is flush right and its scrollbar is 6px, so any right-hand grip lands on it. The number changed because of that, not because 4 was wrong about anything else.
  2. 2The top corners. FINDINGS says four corners at ~12px. At the top right that is a 12px bite out of Close, and a mis-grab there does not close a window, it resizes one. The two top corners are the height of the top edge here, and the bottom two are larger than proposed, because the bottom two are the ones people use.
  3. 3“Both platforms’ chrome is the same” is only true of route A. Route B is often described as cheap, and in JSON it is. In chrome it costs 37px above a 40px bar, on a window whose minimum height is 560 — and the bar it adds is not one we can photograph for a README, because it differs per desktop. That is the argument the second drawing exists to make, and it is why the drawing carries four desktops rather than one.
  4. 4Not checked, and worth one run of the harness. Whether matchMedia’s change event actually fires on WebKitGTK when the portal flips — theme.ts depends on it and the spike only measured the value at launch, not the listener. If it does not fire, “follows your desktop” silently means “follows it once, at startup”, which is a different promise.
  5. 5Not checked, and cheap. Whether startResizeDragging works at all under the harness’s openbox. It maps to gtk_window_begin_resize_drag and the WM advertises _NET_WM_ACTION_RESIZE, so it should — but the whole of route A rests on it, and the harness can already drive a synthetic press-move-release at a corner. That is the one measurement I would take before writing the component.

Not designed, deliberately. The download page, the release tiers and the updater channel — out of scope by the issue. Mobile, the Duo poses and the web client — nothing here reaches them. The menu-bar island, which is macOS-only and has rules that contradict this document on purpose. And no Linux implementation of the tray, the badge or artifact capture: this page says what their absence looks like and stops there.
